Ostriches swallow small stones because they help break down food inside the bird’s muscular gizzard. With no teeth to chew their meals, ostriches do some of that physical processing farther along the digestive tract. A pebble that seems out of place beside a meal can therefore have a useful role inside the bird. [Auckland Zoo](https://www.aucklandzoo.co.nz/animals/common-ostrich).

It is one of those animal facts that sounds stranger than the explanation. To understand it, start with the meal, then follow what happens after the ostrich swallows.

## A meal that needs breaking down

Wild ostriches eat a variety of foods, but leaves, roots, seeds and grasses make up much of their diet. They also take animal foods, including insects. These birds are omnivores, even though plants feature heavily on the menu.

Those meals reach the digestive system without the chewing we associate with teeth. Swallowed grit collects in the gizzard, where it helps with food breakdown. The useful question is less “Why would a bird eat a pebble?” and more “Where does this bird grind its food?” [Auckland Zoo](https://www.aucklandzoo.co.nz/animals/common-ostrich).

## The gizzard supplies the movement

The gizzard is a muscular part of the stomach. Its contractions move food and swallowed stones together, helping grind and mix plant material. Scientists call these stomach stones **gastroliths**.

Think of a mill powered by muscle. That comparison describes the physical action; it does not mean the stones perform the whole job of digestion. [Wings and Sander’s research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2197205/).

## Grinding is one part of digestion

The ostrich stomach has glandular and muscular regions with different jobs. The glandular region supplies secretions involved in chemical digestion, while the gizzard performs mechanical processing.

Breaking food into smaller pieces and chemically digesting it are connected processes, but they are not interchangeable. An anatomical study of ostriches describes this distinction between the stomach’s secretory and muscular functions. [Umar and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11975356/).

There is another stage farther along. Microbial fermentation in the large intestine helps ostriches use fibrous plant material. Research on domesticated ostriches describes grinding and fermentation as complementary parts of this system. The gizzard is therefore an important stop along the route, rather than the endpoint. [Abd El-Wahab and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8342792/).

## Do the stones stay unchanged?

No. In experiments with farmed ostriches, researchers recovered stones after different periods inside the birds and found that they had worn down. Different rock types behaved differently.

That result shows the gizzard is an active environment. It does not establish one fixed lifespan for every stone, and the experiment should not be read as a guide to selecting or supplying grit. [Wings and Sander’s experiment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2197205/).

The next time you hear that ostriches “eat stones”, there is a fuller story behind the phrase: muscle, mechanical grinding, digestive secretions and microbial activity all contribute to processing a meal.
